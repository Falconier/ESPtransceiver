ESPOlivia: a 13 cm transceiver and beacon built from ESP32-S3 Wi-Fi chips

How two inexpensive dev boards, with no added radio hardware, send and receive Morse code, Feld Hellschreiber and Olivia on 2.3–2.4 GHz, and how a third board runs as a standalone beacon. From the chip in plain terms, through the circuit, to every stage of the firmware and the PC software.

Project: Falconier/ESPtransceiverBuilt on: jochenhammes/esp32-sdr-trx v1.0.0-rc4Status as of 22 Oct 2026
Licence required to transmit. Everything here that transmits needs an amateur radio licence covering the frequency, the mode, power limits, identification and (for the beacon) unattended operation. The transmitter is a Wi-Fi chip's test tone: its power is estimated, not calibrated, and its harmonics and spurious emissions have not been measured.

1 · The project in plain terms

Every ESP32-S3 contains a complete 2.4 GHz radio, normally used only for Wi-Fi and Bluetooth. That radio has the same building blocks as a general-purpose radio: an antenna connection, an amplifier, a frequency synthesizer, a mixer and a fast analog-to-digital converter. The open-source project esp32-sdr-trx found that, with the right register settings, the chip can be made to:

  • listen to any frequency from about 1.84 to 2.79 GHz and stream the raw radio signal to a PC, like a software-defined radio (SDR); and
  • transmit a single carrier whose frequency and strength are updated thousands of times per second. That is enough to send Morse code, picture-text (Feld Hell) and multi-tone digital text (Olivia).

This project turns that into working radio stations. Each board is plugged into a PC by USB; a Python program on the PC does all the signal processing, decoding and encoding, and shows a small window to type into and read from. Two boards on the same desk talk to each other like two amateur stations. A third board runs its own firmware with no PC at all and acts as a beacon: every 30 seconds it sends its call sign in Morse code or in Olivia.

AnalogyThink of the chip's radio as a car engine that only ever drives one route (Wi-Fi). The firmware takes the steering wheel away from the Wi-Fi software and drives it by hand: it points the radio at a frequency, opens the throttle (gain) and records what the engine "hears", or makes it hum at one precise pitch and taps that hum on and off in Morse code.

2 · The ESP32-S3, explained

The ESP32-S3 is a microcontroller from Espressif: a small computer on one chip, with its own memory, input/output pins and radio. The boards in this project carry it inside a shielded module with a printed antenna.

The key point: none of this needs extra parts. The antenna on the module, the radio and the USB port are enough for a complete, if very low-power, microwave station.

3 · System architecture

Three boards, three roles. Each station board is driven entirely by its PC program over one USB cable; the beacon board only needs power.

Who does what

  • The board only moves raw data. When receiving, it tunes the radio, samples the signal, filters it down to 62.5 thousand samples per second and streams them to the PC. When transmitting, it plays a list of tiny instructions ("records") from the PC: one frequency offset and one power setting every 125 µs.
  • The PC does everything that needs intelligence: finding the signal (it drifts by kilohertz), turning it into audio, decoding Morse, drawing the Hell picture, decoding Olivia, and, for sending, turning text into records.
  • The beacon is the exception: its firmware generates its own records on the chip, so it needs no PC.

5 · The radio inside the chip

Espressif publishes the radio's behaviour as Wi-Fi, not its circuit. Figure 3 is a functional reconstruction: it shows the blocks a direct-conversion 2.4 GHz transceiver of this kind contains, labelled with what the esp32-sdr-trx firmware actually controls (register names come from that project's research).

Receiving: direct conversion

The receiver mixes the antenna signal with a local oscillator at (nearly) the wanted frequency. Everything near that frequency appears at low frequencies in two signals, I and Q, which together keep the difference between "above" and "below" the LO. The converters sample I and Q 16 million times a second in the build used here, which shows a 16 MHz-wide slice of spectrum around the LO. The firmware then keeps only the narrow part we need.

A direct-conversion receiver always shows a strong line at its own LO frequency (leakage). That line turned out to matter: the PC's frequency tracking once locked onto it instead of a weak signal (section 7).

Transmitting: a steered test tone

Wi-Fi chips contain a calibration mode that emits a plain carrier. The firmware turns it on, then changes two things on every update:

  • Frequency. The PLL frequency is set by a word W; one step of W moves the carrier 457.76 Hz. The firmware rewrites only the lowest byte of W, through the chip's internal analog control bus, 8000 times per second, with second-order noise shaping so that the average frequency lands on a grid 16 times finer (28.6 Hz).
  • Power. A register holds the transmitter's gain code. Codes 64 to 127 span 17.9 dB, measured with a PlutoSDR. Code 64 is the strongest.
fcarrier = 30 MHz × (32 + W/65536) + q4 × 28.61 Hz   ·   q4 ∈ [−672, +672] (±19.2 kHz)   ·   P = Pmax − ΔdB(gain code)

The carrier cannot be switched fully off between Morse elements without restarting the PLL, and the weakest gain code is only 18 dB below the strongest. So during key-up the transmitter also moves the weak carrier 12 kHz away (the space shift), out of the receiver's audio passband.

6 · Firmware

6.1 A bare-metal RAM image

The esp32-sdr-trx firmware has no operating system, no heap and no interrupts: both cores run polling loops. It is built with ESP-IDF v5.5's headers, ROM linker scripts and the vendor PHY library (libphy.a) for calibration, but none of ESP-IDF's runtime. The stations load it into RAM through the boot ROM's download mode each time the PC program starts (esptool load-ram, about 8 s); nothing is written to flash.

This project uses two images built from the same source:

  • Transceiver, decimation 16 (release/esp32-sdr-trx-trx-decim16.bin): receiver plus transmitter, streaming at 62.5 ksps. Windows' USB serial driver carries only about 0.275 MB/s, so upstream's 250 ksps (16-bit) stream lost samples; the extra decimation stage was added here.
  • Beacon (release/esp32-beacon.bin): the same radio code, with the command loop replaced by a beacon program (section 9).

6.2 Receive pipeline on the chip

ADC 16 Msps → 4th-order CIC ÷16 → 1 Msps → FIR ÷16 (81 taps, passband ±15 kHz) → 62.5 ksps → shift → int8 I/Q → USB packets

The converter writes I/Q pairs into a circular ring in SRAM. Both CPU cores take turns on 16,000-pair units, each running its own copy of the decimator from its own instruction memory so they never compete. The CIC filter is computed with the S3's SIMD instructions. Each output packet carries a sequence number and sample index, so the PC can detect lost data. The final output shift scales the result into 8 bits: shift 4 suits boards on the same desk, while a distant signal needs gain 40 with shift 2 (section 10).

6.3 Transmit records

A transmission is a stream of 32-bit records from the PC, played at a fixed rate (8000 per second here):

The chip waits until 4000 records are buffered (the PC keeps about 4800 queued), then keys the carrier and plays one record per tick. It reports buffer fill and underruns every 5 ms. If the PC stops sending for 0.5 s, the firmware drops the carrier by itself; a session also ends after 600 s. Before keying, the PC chooses an LO whose PLL word has its low byte in 48–207, so that offsets never carry into the next byte. That can move the LO up to about 15 kHz from the dial, which the records' offset then makes up.

6.4 Changes this project made to the firmware

  • Decimation 16 (decim16-firmware.patch): the 62.5 ksps stream that Windows can carry without loss.
  • Transmit floor 2300 MHz (from upstream's 2320), for the beacon in the US 2300–2310 MHz segment. The PLL itself covers 2210–2790 MHz.
  • Booting from flash (beacon/firmware-hooks.patch). Upstream only ever ran after a download-mode load, which leaves the chip prepared. A flash boot needed:
    • the timer-group and RTC watchdogs' flash-boot mode cleared (clearing their enable bit alone does not stop them; the chip reset itself every ~0.3 s);
    • the BBPLL's analog control bus enabled and the crystal frequency set, as ESP-IDF's rtc_clk_init() does (otherwise the CPU never reached 240 MHz);
    • RTC_CNTL_BB_I2C_FORCE_PU kept on, so every PHY, PLL and transmitter register write reaches the radio;
    • GPIO0's RTC pad mux and pad hold cleared (after a reset over USB the BOOT button stayed dead until a power cycle).
  • Direct keying (radio_tx_key_on/update/off): lets the beacon play records it generates itself, with the same noise shaping as the PC-fed path.

7 · PC software

The station program is Python (NumPy for signal processing, Tk for the window), packaged as a single executable for Windows and Linux. One process drives one board, half duplex: while it transmits, its receive stream is paused.

Following a drifting signal (AFC)

A cold board drifts 110–190 Hz per second at key-on and settles to about 1 Hz/s after a few minutes; two boards' crystals also disagree by up to ~13 kHz. A Morse decoder listening at one audio pitch would lose the signal within a second. RxAfc therefore looks at the spectrum every 65 ms (a 4096-point FFT, 15 Hz bins) and mixes the carrier to 700 Hz:

  • New signals are found by comparing each spectrum with the median of the last 4 s, which hides the receiver's own fixed spurs. A candidate must appear at the same frequency in two frames in a row: one of ~1000 noise bins passes 10 dB almost every frame, but never twice in one place.
  • Steady carriers (a beacon) become part of that median within seconds, so a locked carrier is also followed directly in the plain spectrum within ±400 Hz, and a very strong one (40 dB) is locked without the median test.
  • Weak carriers are followed slowly and down to 10 dB above the noise, but only within 15 dB of their own recent strength, so noise between a strong station's letters is never mistaken for it.
  • Known non-signals are ignored: the transmitter's own space-shifted carrier (12 kHz away), and the receiver's LO leakage, which sits at the LO's fine-tuning offset rather than at 0 Hz.
  • Olivia and Hell use a hold mode: lock on the steady lead tone, fit its drift as a straight line, and mix along that line through the message.

On this transmitter every mode is built from the same two levers: a frequency offset and a gain code per record. CW changes the gain code (with the space shift during key-up). Hell does the same, faster, with a 1 ms envelope instead of 5 ms. Olivia keeps the gain constant and steps the frequency to a new tone every 32 ms. Because Olivia's 31.25 Hz tone spacing does not fall on the 28.6 Hz record grid, consecutive records carry the rounding remainder forward, so each tone's average frequency is exact.

9 · The standalone beacon

The beacon is the same firmware with its command loop replaced. It calibrates the radio itself, keys the transmitter and generates its 8000 records per second on the chip, so it runs from any USB power source. Its ID (DE W4DQB/EXP) is set at build time; the Olivia version of the ID is encoded on the PC during the build by the station's own Olivia code and stored as a 256-symbol table in instruction RAM, because the image's 8 KB data region was full.

ModeLEDEach 30 s period

Both in turnpurpleCW one period, Olivia the next; carrier off 4 s before each Olivia ID so the receiver sees a fresh lead tone

Start-up sequence, shown on the LED: amber blinking once a second for 5 s (also a check of the CPU clock), white while the radio calibrates, cyan while the transmitter tunes, then the mode's colour, bright while transmitting and dim while not. BOOT: a short press moves to the next mode; a 1.5 s hold pauses (the LED blinks the mode's colour). Blinking blue means the radio could not be set up.

Booting. The image holds RAM segments only and sits at flash address 0, where the boot ROM loads it directly, without ESP-IDF's second-stage bootloader. Getting there needed the four start-up fixes in section 6.4. They were found without a serial console by writing register snapshots and progress markers into RTC memory, which survives a reset, and reading them back with esptool.

10 · Measurements and results

Transmit power

Upstream measured about −35 dBm radiated at the weakest gain code (code 127) with a PlutoSDR at 50 cm, ±10 dB, varying about 10 dB from run to run. Adding the 17.9 dB gain range gives roughly −17 dBm EIRP (≈ 20 µW) at code 64. Harmonics and spurious emissions are unmeasured.

Stability

Cold transmitters drift 110–190 Hz/s at key-on; warm ones about 1 Hz/s. Crystal offsets between boards reach ~13 kHz at 2.4 GHz (about 5 ppm). Upstream measured ±12 Hz over 5 s and up to 2 kHz over minutes.

Range test: beacon at ~30 m

The beacon was placed about 30 m (100 ft) away, outdoors in a tree, through two walls. Findings:

  • An ESP32-C5 running ESP-WebSDR next to the station boards showed the beacon clearly, but its Wi-Fi access point overloaded the station boards' receivers; with it unplugged, the beacon appeared at about 17 dB above the noise per 65 ms frame (20–26 dB averaged over a second), fading 5–10 dB.
  • Default receive settings rounded the weak signal away in the 8-bit stream; gain 40 with output shift 2 fixed that. Gain 60 or more overloaded the receiver.
  • Olivia 16/500 decoded the distant beacon (DE W4DQB/EXP, about one clean ID in three). CW did not with the standard decoder, whose ~200 Hz listening window leaves this signal ~6 dB above the noise. Filtered to ±25 Hz offline, the CW ID stands ~10 dB clear. A narrowband CW decoder that decodes the recording is in review (PR #10), not yet tested live.

Theoretical range

Free-space path loss at 2394 MHz is 100.0 + 20·log10(d/km) dB. With −17 dBm EIRP, isotropic antennas and an ESP32-S3 receiver (noise figure guessed at ~8 dB), CW in a 100 Hz bandwidth reaches about 14 km in free space; a 24 dBi dish at one end multiplies that by about 16. In practice line of sight, Fresnel clearance and obstacles dominate: walls and foliage cost 10–30 dB, which matches the 30 m result above being near the limit for CW.

11 · Limits, safety, band plan

  • Licensing. Transmitting needs an amateur licence. The program asks once for acceptance of upstream's licence notice. Identify with your call sign; the software sends what you type.
  • Unmeasured emissions. Power is an estimate; harmonics and spurs have not been measured. Do not add an amplifier or high-gain antenna without measuring.
  • Band plan used here. Experimental two-way transmissions at 2394.250 MHz; the beacon at 2304.350 MHz (US 2300–2310 MHz segment). The firmware transmits only inside 2300–2450 MHz.
  • Beacon operation. A flashed beacon transmits whenever it has power. Unattended or automatic operation has its own rules in licence conditions.
  • Hardware limits. No receive filtering in front of the chip, so nearby 2.4 GHz transmitters (Wi-Fi) desensitise it; the key-up carrier is only 18 dB down (hence the space shift); frequency drift is large when cold.
  • Tested scope. Three ESP32-S3 boards, one Windows 11 PC; the Linux executable is built and smoke-tested, not run on a board.

A · Glossary and file map

AFCAutomatic frequency control: finding and following the carrier.
CIC / FIRCascaded integrator–comb filter (cheap, coarse decimation) and finite impulse response filter (precise, flat passband).
DecimationLowering the sample rate after filtering, keeping only the wanted bandwidth.
DialThe frequency a receiver is "tuned to"; in upper-sideband (USB) reception a carrier 700 Hz above the dial is heard as a 700 Hz tone.
EIRPEffective isotropic radiated power: transmitter power plus antenna gain.
I/QThe in-phase and quadrature parts of a signal mixed to 0 Hz; together they keep positive and negative frequencies apart.
LOLocal oscillator: the frequency the radio's PLL generates and mixes with.
PLL / SDMPhase-locked loop frequency synthesizer; its sigma-delta modulator sets the fractional part of the multiplication.
RecordOne 32-bit transmit instruction: frequency offset, gain code, flags.
Space shiftMoving the weak key-up carrier 12 kHz away so the receiver hears silence between Morse elements.
WPMWords per minute of Morse ("PARIS" timing: dot = 1.2 s / WPM).
